# Relationship science for writing and ordering couples prompts

Method note: built from web search results (abstracts, PubMed entries, university press releases, institute pages). Full-text fetch was blocked in this session (stafforini.com did not resolve), so most effect sizes below come from abstracts or press summaries, not the papers' tables. Where a figure rests on a secondary source, that is stated.

## 1. Aron et al. 1997 "36 questions": what made it work, replications and limits

### Takeaway
Closeness came from structured self-disclosure that escalates in intimacy across three sets, is reciprocal (turn-taking), sustained (about 45 minutes) and personalistic, compared with a small-talk control. The original and most follow-ups used strangers; the couple evidence is thinner (conference studies with about 150 couples) and points to partner responsiveness as the active ingredient.

### Cited Findings
- Aron, Melinat, Aron, Vallone & Bator (1997), *PSPB* 23(4), 363-377: 36 self-disclosure prompts in three sets that escalate in intimacy and personalism, about 45 minutes, compared with a validated small-talk task; closeness measured with the Inclusion of Other in the Self (IOS) scale. — [Bibliographic record](https://bibbase.org/network/publication/aron-melinat-aron-vallone-bator-theexperimentalgenerationofinterpersonalclosenessaprocedureandsomepreliminaryfindings-1997); [summary](https://scholarhub.vn/publication/The-Experimental-Generation-of-Interpersonal-Closeness-A-Procedure-and-Some-Preliminary-Findings/a1d29998-eefa-4ec0-bb8c-25459abdb441)
- Later researchers often use shortened versions of the procedure (fewer questions, less time). — [Search summary of later use, JSPR 2021](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0265407521996055)
- Reciprocity is causal, not incidental: Sprecher et al. (2013, *JESP* 49, 860-866) randomly assigned stranger pairs to take turns disclosing vs one person disclosing then the other; turn-taking produced more liking, closeness, perceived similarity and enjoyment, and the advantage persisted after a second interaction even though the non-reciprocal pairs swapped roles. — [Sage record](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0265407512459033); [Psychology Today summary](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/fulfillment-at-any-age/201404/the-secret-to-revealing-your-secrets)
- Couples: Welker & Slatcher (2014, SPSP conference; two studies, about 150 couples) had two couples do the Fast Friends (36-question style) task together; couples who did the escalating version reported more passionate love for their own partner than couples who made small talk, and how responsive the other couple was predicted the rise in passionate love. Effect sizes and a journal citation were not in the press coverage. — [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2014/02/140210114544.htm); [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/812115)
- Deep questions beat expectations: Kardas, Kumar & Epley (2022, *JPSP*; 12 experiments, 1,800+ participants, mostly strangers) found people overestimate how awkward deep conversations will be and underestimate how much their partner cares about their answers; deep talks were more enjoyable and connecting, and people who did both shallow and deep talks preferred the deep one despite predicting the opposite. — [APA press release](https://www.apa.org/news/press/releases/2021/09/deep-conversations-strangers); [Chicago Booth Review](https://www.chicagobooth.edu/review/deep-conversations-strangers-are-more-fulfilling-small-talk)
- Disclosure and liking are linked in three directions (we like people who disclose to us, we disclose to people we like, and we like people we have disclosed to): Collins & Miller (1994) meta-analysis, *Psychological Bulletin* 116(3). Exact effect sizes not retrieved. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7809308/)

### Inferences
- Order the deck as the 36 questions do: light and personal first, then values and memories, then vulnerable and partner-directed ("what I appreciate about you"). The escalation, not any single question, is the mechanism.
- Make reciprocity structural: both partners answer every talk card, alternating who goes first. "Then ask me back" is supported by Sprecher 2013.
- For established couples the gain is less about "getting to know" and more about novelty and feeling responded to. Prompts should seek things the partner probably doesn't know yet (forgotten memories, changing opinions, hypotheticals), otherwise the novelty effect is lost.
- Kardas et al. suggest players will underrate deep cards in advance; a gentle ramp and an easy pass lower the anticipated awkwardness that keeps people shallow.

### Gaps
- Could not open the 1997 full text, so the per-study samples, effect sizes and the dating-couple comparison data are unverified here. (Commonly repeated claims, such as the participant pair who later married, should not be cited without the paper.)
- Found no peer-reviewed, preregistered replication of the 36 questions within existing couples (one partner pair answering alone). Welker & Slatcher used couple-pairs, not a single couple.

## 2. Gottman: love maps, bids, fondness and admiration

### Takeaway
Gottman's most-cited number: newlyweds still married six years later had turned toward each other's bids about 86% of the time in the lab, those divorced about 33%. Question prompts map naturally onto "love maps" (knowing the partner's inner world) and "fondness and admiration" (voiced appreciation); bids map onto small reactions and answers during play.

### Cited Findings
- Couples still married at six years turned toward bids 86% of the time in the lab; those divorced, 33%. A "bid" is any small attempt at connection; responding is "turning toward", ignoring is "turning away". — [Gottman Institute](https://www.gottman.com/blog/turn-toward-instead-of-away/); [Gottman Institute, bids and trust](https://www.gottman.com/blog/an-introduction-to-emotional-bids-and-trust/)
- Some summaries credit the study to Gottman with Janice Driver; the Gottman Institute pages credit Gottman. The original peer-reviewed paper was not located in this search. — [Mount Hermon summary](https://blog.mounthermon.org/family-adult/2020/03/making-marriage-last/)

### Inferences
- Love-map cards: "What's something you're looking forward to this month?", "What's a worry you haven't said out loud?", plus "guess my answer" cards that test and update the map (low stakes if framed as play, not a quiz to fail).
- Fondness and admiration cards: specific appreciations ("a moment this week I liked you a lot"). These are low risk and good warm-ups before vulnerable cards.
- Reactions and "then ask me back" are digital turning-toward: every answer should get a response, so the game should make responding easy (reactions, a follow-up line), never leave an answer hanging.

### Gaps
- The 86/33 figures rest on institute and secondary summaries; no peer-reviewed citation or sample size was found in this session. Gottman's research is largely from his own lab and not independently replicated in all respects.

## 3. Responsiveness (Reis & Shaver intimacy process model)

### Takeaway
Intimacy forms when a disclosure is met by a response that leaves the discloser feeling understood, validated and cared for. In couples' daily lives, responsiveness predicts intimacy more strongly than disclosure itself, and experimentally it raises sexual desire (especially in women). Responses to good news matter as much as, or more than, support in bad times.

### Cited Findings
- Laurenceau, Barrett & Pietromonaco (1998, *JPSP* 74(5), 1238-1251), event-contingent diaries over 1-2 weeks: own disclosure and partner disclosure both predicted intimacy, partly mediated by perceived partner responsiveness. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9599440/); [PDF](https://www.affective-science.org/pubs/1998/LaurenFBPl1998.pdf)
- Laurenceau et al. (2005), daily diaries of married couples: partner responsiveness was a stronger predictor of intimacy than self-disclosure. — [PDF](https://www.affective-science.org/pubs/2005/Laurenceauetal2005.pdf)
- Birnbaum, Reis et al. (2016, *JPSP* 111(4), 530-546), three studies (online experiment with a responsive vs unresponsive confederate "partner"; coded face-to-face interactions; 100 couples, 6-week diary): responsiveness increased sexual desire, more strongly for women; feeling special and seeing the partner as valuable explained the link. Desire was self-reported. — [BGU record](https://cris.bgu.ac.il/en/publications/intimately-connected-the-importance-of-partner-responsiveness-for/); [ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2016/07/160720215315.htm)
- Gable, Gonzaga & Strachman (2006, *JPSP*): responsiveness when a partner shares good news predicted relationship quality better than responsiveness during negative-event talks, now and later; daily responses to good news predicted well-being better than social support. Four response types: active-constructive (enthusiastic), passive-constructive, active-destructive, passive-destructive. — [Wikipedia, positive psychology of relationships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_psychology_of_relationships); [URMC](https://www.urmc.rochester.edu/behavioral-health-partners/bhp-blog/february-2024/good-news-sharing-and-responding.aspx)
- Responsiveness also predicted passionate love in the couple Fast Friends study. — [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2014/02/140210114544.htm)

### Inferences
- The listener's half matters more than the speaker's. Cards can include a listening cue ("Ask one follow-up question before you answer yours", "Tell them what you heard").
- Include "good news" cards ("Something you did lately that you're proud of") that invite enthusiastic responding; they are high-yield and low-risk.
- Responsiveness bridges intimacy and desire (Birnbaum), so warm talk levels are a legitimate on-ramp to sensual levels rather than a detour.

### Gaps
- Gable 2006 exact sample and effect sizes not retrieved (doi 10.1037/0022-3514.91.5.904).
- No study found that tests written "listening prompts" in a game format.

## 4. Self-expansion, novelty, mystery, play and humor

### Takeaway
Doing novel, mildly arousing things together raises relationship quality (lab experiments) and sexual desire (diary and experimental work). Desire also benefits from a sense of the partner as separate and seen anew (Perel: theory, not data). Shared, created humor tracks with satisfaction far more than one partner being funny; mocking humor does the opposite.

### Cited Findings
- Aron, Norman, Aron, McKenna & Heyman (2000, *JPSP*): in 3 experiments, couples doing a 7-minute novel and arousing task together showed bigger before-to-after gains in experienced relationship quality than couples doing a mundane task; results held controlling for social desirability. Survey data linked relationship quality to the number of exciting activities shared. Samples and effect sizes not retrieved. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10707334/)
- Muise et al. (2019, *JPSP*, "Broadening your horizons"): three studies. Diary study of 122 couples together 2+ years over 21 days: desire was higher on days with more self-expansion with the partner; self-expansion via desire linked to more likely and more satisfying sex and higher relationship satisfaction. An experiment that prompted self-expanding activities raised desire. — [York University](https://www.yorku.ca/research/2019/10/04/broadening-horizons-key-to-romantic-satisfaction-new-research-finds-2/); [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/try-something-new-together-research-shows-engaging-self-expanding-activities)
- Limit: a York thesis (Uppot, 2025; 170 early-stage couples) found self-expansion went with higher desire but did not protect against declines over time. — [YorkSpace](https://yorkspace.library.yorku.ca/items/5c39f37e-19f1-4ec8-a6a0-28c9ce1f409f/full)
- Harasymchuk, Walker, Muise & Impett (2021, *JSPR*): people with approach relationship goals got more closeness from date nights, partly because they planned more exciting, self-expanding dates. — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8170361/)
- Perel (*Mating in Captivity*): "love seeks closeness, desire needs distance"; familiarity and caretaking can dampen erotic interest, which feeds on novelty, mystery, autonomy and play. This is clinical theory, not tested data; exact quotes should be checked against the book. — [SuperSummary overview](https://www.supersummary.com/mating-in-captivity/key-figures/)
- Birnbaum et al. 2016 frame the "intimacy-desire paradox" and conclude intimacy feeds desire when it makes the partner feel special and valued, not by mere familiarity. — [BGU record](https://cris.bgu.ac.il/en/publications/intimately-connected-the-importance-of-partner-responsiveness-for/)
- Hall (2017, *Personal Relationships*), meta-analysis of 43 samples, 15,177 participants: creating and sharing humor with a partner was much more strongly linked to satisfaction than being funny in general; all 6 positive humor types correlated positively, 4 of 5 negative types (including mocking and self-defeating) negatively. Correlational. — [KU News](https://news.ku.edu/news/article/2017/02/08/relationship-success-tied-not-joking-shared-sense-humor); [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/study-finds-good-humor-linked-good-relationships/)
- Playfulness between partners has been framed as a bonding mechanism; Carleton's relationship lab summarises benefits of play in established couples. — [Carleton](https://carleton.ca/mental-health/2021/the-benefits-of-play-in-established-romantic-relationships/)

### Inferences
- Mix "do" cards (small novel challenges: a 60-second dance, describe a dream trip in detail, a silly role swap) between talk cards. Aron 2000 used only 7 minutes, so short tasks are enough.
- Mystery and anticipation: face-down cards, sealed answers revealed together and "guess what I'll say" mechanics fit Perel's "seeing the partner anew" and keep the partner a little unknown.
- Seeing the partner in their element: prompts like "When did you last see me be good at something?" target Perel's point that desire rises when the partner is seen from a slight distance.
- Write humor that is shared and absurd (inside jokes, silly hypotheticals), never at a partner's expense.

### Gaps
- No direct experimental data found on anticipation in couples (e.g., a scheduled "envelope" reveal) or on "mystery" mechanics; Perel's claims are theory.
- Some research suggests comfort can matter as much as novelty for some couples (attachment-dependent); only a secondary blog surfaced this, so it is unverified.

## 5. Sexual communication, consent, graded exposure, pressure

### Takeaway
Talking about sex correlates with better sexual function (r = .35 overall, 48 studies), more so in married couples. The main barrier is fear of self-exposure, so lowering the vulnerability of the first disclosure is the design lever. Sex pursued for approach reasons (closeness, pleasure) builds desire and satisfaction; sex to avoid disappointing a partner erodes them, which argues against any pressure. Sensate focus provides a clinical model for graded, non-demand touch.

### Cited Findings
- Mallory, Stanton & Handy (2019, *J Sex Research* 56(7)), meta-analysis of 48 studies, 12,145 participants: sexual communication correlated with overall sexual function r = .35, orgasm r = .23, arousal r = .21, smaller links with desire; stronger in married (r = .47) than dating (r = .11) samples; correlational. — [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/couples-who-communicate-more-about-sex-tend-to-have-better-sex-study-finds); [DeepDyve](https://www.deepdyve.com/lp/taylor-francis/couples-sexual-communication-and-dimensions-of-sexual-function-a-meta-8KmhPqmLJj)
- Rehman et al. (2018, JSPR): the strongest reason people avoid sexual talk is the threat to themselves (self-exposure), more than fear of hurting the partner or the relationship. — [Psychology Today (Rehman lab summary)](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/talking-apes/201810/why-you-wont-talk-about-sexual-issues-with-your-partner)
- Rehman et al. (2017, *Arch Sex Behav*, 115 couples): partners were warmer and more cautious in sexual than nonsexual disagreements but more anxious beforehand. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s10508-017-1006-5)
- Impett, Peplau & Gable (2005): approach sexual motives linked to personal and relationship well-being, avoidance motives negatively. Impett et al. (2008, *JPSP*): approach goals buffered against declines in desire over time. Muise et al. (2013/2017): avoidance goals (to avoid a partner's disappointment) reduce satisfaction; an experiment prompting approach goals raised desire and satisfaction. — [Impett, Peplau & Gable 2005 PDF](https://peplau.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/141/2017/07/Impett_Peplau_Gable_2005.pdf); [Impett et al. 2008 PDF](https://elifinkel.squarespace.com/s/35_ImpettStrachmentFinkelGable2008_JPSP.pdf)
- Sensate focus (Masters & Johnson): staged touch that begins with no breasts, genitals or intercourse and adds areas step by step, focused on sensation rather than performance. Reviewed as well established for some dysfunctions (Heiman & Meston); Avery-Clark, Weiner & Adams-Clark (2019) split it into SF1 for dysfunctions and SF2 for "optimizing intimacy"; the evidence base is described as limited and partly unpublished. — [Springer Medicine (2019 review)](https://www.springermedicine.com/sensate-focus-for-sexual-concerns-an-updated-critical-literature/21958914); [Linschoten et al. 2016](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/14681994.2015.1127909)

### Inferences
- Ask about wants indirectly before directly: private yes/maybe/not-tonight votes where only matches are revealed fit the "self-exposure" barrier (nobody risks being seen as wanting something the partner rejects). This matches the game's existing design.
- Write sensual cards as non-demand, sensation-first (sensate-focus style): attention, slowness, describing sensations, with no goal or "finish". Grade them: clothed touch and words before anything more.
- Avoid any framing that makes saying yes a favour to the partner ("do it for them"); approach framing ("what would feel good to you") is better for desire.
- Married or long-term couples may benefit most from communication cards (larger correlations).

### Gaps
- Most sexual-communication evidence is correlational; no randomized test of a card or game format was found.
- No good efficacy data on sensate focus for non-clinical couples ("optimizing intimacy").

## 6. Risks: heavy disclosure too soon, comparison, conflict triggers, and how to soften

### Takeaway
The evidence base favours gradual escalation and reciprocity; abrupt, one-sided or negative content is where things go wrong. Sexual topics carry anticipatory anxiety; negative behaviour during problem talks hurts satisfaction. The safest design is a ramp, matched depth, positive framing and an easy, unpunished exit.

### Cited Findings
- The 36-question method's escalation is part of its design (three sets of increasing intimacy). — [Bibliographic record](https://bibbase.org/network/publication/aron-melinat-aron-vallone-bator-theexperimentalgenerationofinterpersonalclosenessaprocedureandsomepreliminaryfindings-1997)
- Non-reciprocal disclosure produced less liking than turn-taking. — [Sage record](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0265407512459033)
- People feel more anxiety before sexual conflict talks than nonsexual ones. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s10508-017-1006-5)
- In a small pilot (15 newlywed couples), negative behaviour while discussing a sexual problem was linked to lower satisfaction for wives (tentative). — [Search summary of Rehman lab work](https://uwaterloo.ca/relationship-lab/select-publications)
- Active-destructive and passive-destructive responses to a partner's news harm relationship well-being. — [URMC](https://www.urmc.rochester.edu/behavioral-health-partners/bhp-blog/february-2024/good-news-sharing-and-responding.aspx)
- Mocking and self-defeating humor are linked to lower satisfaction. — [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/study-finds-good-humor-linked-good-relationships/)
- Avoidance-motivated sex (to avoid letting a partner down) lowers satisfaction. — [Impett et al. 2008 PDF](https://elifinkel.squarespace.com/s/35_ImpettStrachmentFinkelGable2008_JPSP.pdf)

### Inferences
- Avoid comparison prompts (exes, "best ever", rating the partner against others or against past sex) and prompts that invite grievances ("what annoys you about me"). Reframe as wishes: "one small thing that would make our weeknights better" rather than "what do I do wrong".
- Keep the heaviest disclosure for late in the night and late in the level arc, and let couples stop at any time without penalty.
- Prefer present/future over past for vulnerable topics, and appreciation before requests.
- Give listener cues on vulnerable cards ("just listen, no fixing").

### Gaps
- Collins & Miller effect sizes, and specific evidence that overly intimate early disclosure reduces liking, were not retrieved in this session.
- No research found on "comparison" prompts specifically; the inference rests on the humor and responsiveness findings.
