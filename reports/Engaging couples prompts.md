# Write prompts that escalate, reciprocate and land

Couples prompts become engaging when they are **short, specific questions or tasks about the two players, raised in intensity a step at a time, answered by both partners in turn, and met with a response that makes the speaker feel heard**. That one sentence carries most of the evidence. Aron's 36 questions built closeness through escalating, reciprocal disclosure. Turn-taking itself causes more liking than one-sided disclosure. In couples' daily lives, the partner's responsiveness predicts intimacy better than the disclosure does. The best-loved commercial decks (We're Not Really Strangers, Esther Perel's *Where Should We Begin*) apply the same recipe in 6 to 10 words a card, break it up with "do this together" wildcards, and close with a dedicated final card. For Tandem's 12- and 16-card nights, the evidence points to an interest curve rather than a ramp: a playful hook within the first two minutes, a sawtooth build with rest beats, **one summit instead of a two- or three-card plateau**, and a cool-down that falls in intensity while it rises in warmth, ending on a reveal. That ending matters most, because memory of an experience tracks its peak and its end far more than its length. Measured against those findings, Tandem's 467 free cards (424 together, 43 apart) plus 40 Couple Pass cards get the content rules right: no exes, no grievances, receiver-led touch, consent built in. The weak spots are structural. **The ending pools are tiny (4 closers, 6 envelopes, 3 twists each way)**, so the most memorable minutes repeat soonest. Levels 1 to 3 lean on templated frames. Level 3 talk cards rarely hand the turn back. Level 7 scenes run past the 25-word ceiling. And one L6 night can stack several 5- to 10-minute sessions. The sections below give the evidence, then a pacing plan, card formats, writing rules with example prompts for each level, and an audit checklist to run against the deck.

## Escalation, turn-taking and a good listener do the work

The best-tested recipe for closeness comes from Aron, Melinat, Aron, Vallone and Bator (1997). It uses 36 self-disclosure prompts in three sets that **rise in intimacy and personal focus over about 45 minutes**, compared against a small-talk control ([bibliographic record](https://bibbase.org/network/publication/aron-melinat-aron-vallone-bator-theexperimentalgenerationofinterpersonalclosenessaprocedureandsomepreliminaryfindings-1997)). The procedure gives about 15 minutes per set and alternates who answers first. The last set turns to "we" statements and telling the partner what you like about them ([Greater Good in Action](https://ggia.berkeley.edu/practice/36_questions_for_increasing_closeness)). Aron's own warning sets the limit: "If you say too much too fast, it puts the person off" ([A More Beautiful Question](https://amorebeautifulquestion.com/36-questions/)). The mechanism is the escalation, not any single question.

Reciprocity is causal, not decoration. Sprecher and colleagues (2013) randomly assigned pairs either to take turns disclosing or to have one person disclose and then the other. **Turn-taking produced more liking, closeness, perceived similarity and enjoyment.** The advantage lasted even after the one-sided pairs swapped roles ([Sage](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0265407512459033)). Players also misjudge depth in advance. Across 12 experiments with over 1,800 people, Kardas, Kumar and Epley found that people overestimate how awkward deep conversations will be and underestimate how much the other person cares about their answers. People who had both kinds of conversation preferred the deep one, despite predicting the opposite ([APA](https://www.apa.org/news/press/releases/2021/09/deep-conversations-strangers)). The design implication is that players will undervalue deep cards before they see them, so a gentle ramp and a one-tap Pass matter more than softer questions do.

The listener's half matters as much as the speaker's. In diary studies, partner disclosure and own disclosure both predicted intimacy, partly through perceived responsiveness ([Laurenceau et al. 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9599440/)). In married couples, **responsiveness predicted intimacy more strongly than self-disclosure** ([Laurenceau et al. 2005](https://www.affective-science.org/pubs/2005/Laurenceauetal2005.pdf)). Responsiveness is also a bridge to desire. Birnbaum, Reis and colleagues found across three studies, including a six-week diary of 100 couples, that responsiveness raised sexual desire, more strongly for women, because it made the partner feel special ([BGU](https://cris.bgu.ac.il/en/publications/intimately-connected-the-importance-of-partner-responsiveness-for/)). Good news is a high-yield, low-risk entry point. Gable, Gonzaga and Strachman found that how a partner responds to good news predicted relationship quality better than how they respond during negative talks ([URMC summary](https://www.urmc.rochester.edu/behavioral-health-partners/bhp-blog/february-2024/good-news-sharing-and-responding.aspx)). The one real-couples test of the 36-question format points the same way. When two couples did the escalating task together, they reported more passionate love for their own partner than couples who made small talk, and the other couple's responsiveness predicted the rise ([ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2014/02/140210114544.htm)).

Novelty and play keep established couples interested. In three experiments, couples who did a **7-minute novel, mildly arousing task** together gained more in relationship quality than couples doing a mundane task ([Aron et al. 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10707334/)). In a 21-day diary of 122 long-term couples, desire was higher on days with more shared self-expansion ([York University](https://www.yorku.ca/research/2019/10/04/broadening-horizons-key-to-romantic-satisfaction-new-research-finds-2/)). A 2025 thesis with 170 early-stage couples found that self-expansion did not stop desire from declining over time ([YorkSpace](https://yorkspace.library.yorku.ca/items/5c39f37e-19f1-4ec8-a6a0-28c9ce1f409f/full)), so novelty helps on the night but is no vaccine. Humor needs care. A meta-analysis of 43 samples (15,177 people) linked **creating and sharing humor with a partner** to satisfaction far more strongly than being funny in general. Mocking and self-defeating humor went the other way ([KU News](https://news.ku.edu/news/article/2017/02/08/relationship-success-tied-not-joking-shared-sense-humor)).

Sexual content brings a specific barrier and a specific remedy. Across 48 studies, sexual communication correlated with sexual function at r = .35, and at **r = .47 in married samples versus .11 in dating samples** ([PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/couples-who-communicate-more-about-sex-tend-to-have-better-sex-study-finds)). The main reason people avoid sexual talk is fear of exposing themselves, more than fear of hurting the partner ([Psychology Today, Rehman lab](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/talking-apes/201810/why-you-wont-talk-about-sexual-issues-with-your-partner)). That is exactly what private yes / maybe / not-tonight votes with match-only reveals remove. Motive matters too. Sex pursued for closeness or pleasure builds desire, while sex pursued to avoid a partner's disappointment erodes satisfaction ([Impett et al. 2008](https://elifinkel.squarespace.com/s/35_ImpettStrachmentFinkelGable2008_JPSP.pdf)). So no card should frame yes as a favour. Sensate focus gives a clinical template for graded, non-demand touch: staged, sensation-first, adding areas step by step, with no performance goal. The evidence for non-clinical "optimizing intimacy" use is limited ([Springer Medicine review](https://www.springermedicine.com/sensate-focus-for-sexual-concerns-an-updated-critical-literature/21958914)).

Two honest limits apply. Most of this evidence comes from strangers or is correlational, and **no study has tested written prompts in a couples game format**. The Gottman figure often quoted here (couples still married after six years turned toward each other's bids 86% of the time, divorced couples 33%) comes from institute summaries rather than a located paper ([Gottman Institute](https://www.gottman.com/blog/turn-toward-instead-of-away/)). Treat it as directional.

## Loved decks ask about "us" in under fifteen words

The decks people praise share a clear voice. Verifiable We're Not Really Strangers couples prompts are 7 to 9 words, in the second person, about the two players: "What was your first impression of me?" and "Do you think I've ever had my heart broken?" (user-made flashcards, unverified against the print deck) ([Brainscape](https://www.brainscape.com/flashcards/wnrs-couples-edition-relationships-expan-18241911/packs/22719288)). The Couples Edition is about 150 cards in three escalating levels, with wildcards. Reviewers praise questions "you wouldn't normally think to ask" ([Thingtesting](https://thingtesting.com/brands/were-not-really-strangers/reviews?p=2)). Its "Perception" cards have one partner guess about the other, so the guess is the disclosure and the partner confirms or corrects it. Its wildcards are short joint tasks: write down and compare "who is most likely to make us late", each rate 1 to 10 how well the other knows them, draw each other in a minute ([Brainscape](https://www.brainscape.com/flashcards/wnrs-couples-edition-relationships-expan-18241911/packs/22719288)). Perel takes a different route. Her cards are **unfinished story starters** ("My most irrational fear...", "The last time I felt lost..."), so the player picks which story to tell and sets the depth themselves ([Bustle](https://www.bustle.com/wellness/esther-perel-game-where-should-we-begin-help-couples)). Her game also offers a story-cards-only mode for a first or casual night ([Perel, how to play](https://estherperel.com/how-to-play-where-should-we-begin)). Design analyses of WNRS credit its prewritten escalating prompts with a clearer flow than Truth or Dare, and single out its final card as key to engagement ([Mechanics of Magic](https://mechanicsofmagic.com/?p=27146)).

The complaints are just as consistent. Paired users say questions repeat after a few months, "the same five themes rephrased" ([Habi](https://habi.app/insights/best-couple-apps/)). One truth-or-dare reviewer said the app "kept cycling through roughly 30" dares ([JustUseApp](https://justuseapp.com/en/app/933524873/dirty-sex-game-for-couples/reviews)). Desire users said the dares became "almost all very vanilla now" and complained they "can't even search dares by spicy level" ([App Store reviews](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/desire-couples-game/id923073855?see-all=reviews&platform=iphone)). Content can also miss in both directions. Some couples find WNRS's third level too heavy for newer relationships, while for deeply connected couples "some questions might feel too simplistic" ([therapist guide](https://ofourkind.squarespace.com/blog/a-therapists-guide-to-the-best-couples-card-games-5-tools-for-deeper-intimacy)). The And sells a separate Long-Term edition for couples together three or more years ([desertcart](https://www.desertcart.nl/products/354452827-the-and-long-term-couples-edition)). BestSelf's topic-sorted deck was called "repetitive and kind of basic" ([desertcart](https://www.desertcart.nl/products/164928945-intimacy-deck)).

Answer formats matter as much as the questions. Paired reveals both partners' answers only after both submit, and its guide calls that reveal the "discovery moment" ([Android Authority guide](https://cellphoneplans.androidauthority.com/CellPhones/Guides/paired-app-couples-relationship-questions)). Spicer and Kindu collect private yes / maybe / no marks and show only mutual matches, so any "no" stays hidden ([DatingAdvice](https://www.datingadvice.com/for-women/spicer-helps-couples-express-their-desires)). Tandem's two phones can do both natively: sealed answers that open together, and private votes. A single physical deck can do neither. The inference is that **varying how people answer (say it, seal it, rate it, draw it, act it) refreshes a night more than adding more questions on the same themes**. No source ranks these formats by player preference, so the mix below is a design judgement.

## Shape the night as a sawtooth with one summit and a warm landing

Pacing theory and memory research agree. Schell's interest-curve lens asks whether an experience opens with a hook, builds with rest periods and ends with a finale ([Lens of the Interest Curve](https://gamefroot-developer.gitbooks.io/jesse-schell-the-art-of-game-design-lenses-1-to-1/content/061.html)). A meta-analysis of 174 effect sizes found the peak-end effect on overall evaluations was large, **r ≈ 0.58**, about as strong as the plain average, while duration had essentially no effect ([RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jobhdp/v170y2022ics0749597822000334.html)). Endings can dilute as well as lift. Children given one desirable candy were more satisfied than children given the same candy plus a lesser one ([Psychology Today](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/fulfillment-any-age/201209/happiness-it-s-all-about-the-ending)). One counterpoint: simple, short positive experiences may show no peak-end effect at all ([Mah & Bernstein 2019](https://www.lifespancognition.com/s/mah2019.pdf)). A 12-card night is short, so the average quality of every card still counts. Escape-room designers warn against long plateaus and often write the ending first ([Sherlocked](https://www.sherlocked.nl/blog/how-escape-rooms-are-designed)). Improv warm-ups exist to lower stakes before risk ([Jimmy Carrane](https://jimmycarrane.com/why-warm-up-games-should-be-part-of-every-improv-class/)). Jackbox's rule is one task at a time, and its designers added "the reveal" when a final round fell flat ([Built In](https://builtin.com/media-gaming/jackbox-games-design-party-pack)).

Tandem's `plan()` in `server/game.ts` already does much of this. Slots are offsets below the night's ceiling (the lower of the two partners' picks). A 12-card night runs warm-up 3,2,3, build 2,1,2,1, peak 0,0 and cool-down 3,2,3. A 16-card night runs 3,2,3,2 / 2,1,2,1,2,1 / 0,0,0 / 3,2,3. The mystery card falls on the third build card. A twist fires with 28% probability once six cards have passed since the last one, and a "down" twist is forced at the start of the cool-down. The sawtooth build is right. Four things fight the curve. The peak is a **plateau** of two or three ceiling cards. The cool-down rises back to offset 2 instead of landing. Some nights get no "up" twist at all. And nothing ensures a reveal in the last card before the closer. The proposal below keeps the build, makes one card the summit and turns the landing into a gift. The phase timings are estimates (about 2 minutes per talk card), not sourced figures.

| Night | Warm-up | Build | Summit | Cool-down | Then |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 12 cards (default, "about 30 min") | 3, 3, 2. Card 1 is a playful Guess or This-or-that, never gated, so the first laugh lands within about 2 minutes | 2, 1, 2, 1. Mystery on card 5 or 6. One guaranteed "up" twist between cards 5 and 8 | 0, 0, but the two must differ in format: a talk or Choose card at the ceiling, then the one hands-on summit | 2, 3, 3. Card 12 is an Appreciate or sealed Guess card, so the landing carries a reveal | Closer (one look-back question plus a "button" when together), then the envelope |
| 16 cards ("about 45 min") | 3, 3, 2, 2 | 2, 1, 2, 1, 2, 1. One guaranteed "up" twist between cards 7 and 11 | 0, 1, 0. The middle card is a breath card, so the night has one clear summit, not a ridge | 2, 3, 3 with the existing "down" twist at its start. The last card is sealed or appreciative | Same |

A few rules go with the table. **Allow at most one card with a timer of 5 minutes or more per night, placed at the summit.** Level 6 has 19 such cards out of 87, including 10-minute sessions, so an L6 ceiling night can quietly run past an hour. At ceilings 1 to 2, every offset clamps to Level 1, so the curve has to come from format rather than level: guess, then story starter, then a sealed appreciation. Rotate the night's shape on the map ("classic ramp", "slow burn" with one summit card, "two-hump", "story night" on one theme), keeping the warm-up, closer and envelope fixed. The shape is what players feel, and varying it costs no new cards. A night where the summit card is passed must still feel complete. That is why the closer and envelope, not the summit, should carry the "end" of the peak-end rule.

The closing needs three beats, drawn from facilitation practice and WNRS. Facilitators advise one concrete closing question, a turn for each person and no commentary mid-round, and they note that the common failure is skipping the close when a session runs long ([Facilitate Better](https://facilitatebetter.substack.com/p/winding-down-how-to-better-end-meetings?r=30cgo2)). WNRS sets its Final Card aside at the start, and each player writes a message to be opened later ([Official Game Rules](https://officialgamerules.org/game-rules/were-not-really-strangers/)). Gottman's "rituals of connection" draw their value from shared meaning more than from the activity ([Gottman Institute](https://www.gottman.com/blog/the-little-things-that-keep-love-strong/)). Beat one looks back: one specific appreciation each ("a moment tonight you'll keep"). Beat two turns toward each other: a hug or held eye contact, or a spoken line on video when apart. Beat three looks forward: the envelope, opened together, holding something both partners contributed and a small take-away for the week. It must never be a task with a deadline. The envelope should feel at least as warm as the last card, or it dilutes the memory. Shop links, which appear after the envelope, belong visually below and apart from it, so they never become the remembered end.

## Twelve card formats, fourteen writing rules and examples for every level

### Formats that use both phones

Each format below has a job in the curve. The two-phone mechanics (seal, private vote, mystery flip) are what a printed deck cannot copy, so they should appear on most nights.

| Format | How it plays | Why it works | Best levels and phase |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ask (reciprocal) | One question, both answer, first speaker alternates | Turn-taking causes liking ([Sage](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0265407512459033)) | All, the backbone of the build |
| Story starter | "Finish the sentence: The last time I..." | Teller sets the depth; low pressure for reluctant partners ([Bustle](https://www.bustle.com/wellness/esther-perel-game-where-should-we-begin-help-couples)) | L1 to 5, warm-up and build |
| Guess, sealed | Both seal a guess about the other, open together, then the guessed partner explains | The guess is the disclosure; the simultaneous reveal is a mini peak ([Android Authority](https://cellphoneplans.androidauthority.com/CellPhones/Guides/paired-app-couples-relationship-questions)) | L1 to 3, and the last card before the closer |
| Rate and compare | Both seal a 1 to 10 rating, reveal, explain the gap | WNRS wildcard staple; the gap is the conversation ([Brainscape](https://www.brainscape.com/flashcards/wnrs-couples-edition-relationships-expan-18241911/packs/22719288)) | L1 to 2, about yourself or "us", never rating the partner against anyone |
| This or that | Two options, guess the partner's pick first | Fast, laughable, ideal hook | L1, card 1 |
| Good news | "Something you did lately you're proud of"; the listener asks two questions first | Responses to good news predict quality ([URMC](https://www.urmc.rochester.edu/behavioral-health-partners/bhp-blog/february-2024/good-news-sharing-and-responding.aspx)) | L1 to 2, warm-up |
| Appreciate | One specific thing, about a recent moment | Fondness and Aron's Set III; warms the landing | All, cool-down |
| Do (short novel task) | Under 2 minutes, playful, together | 7 minutes of novelty lifted quality ([Aron 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10707334/)) | L1 to 5, mid-build twist |
| Choose (private vote) | Each privately picks; only matches show | Removes the self-exposure barrier ([Rehman lab](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/talking-apes/201810/why-you-wont-talk-about-sexual-issues-with-your-partner)) | L4 to 8, before the summit |
| Session (non-demand touch) | Receiver-led, sensation-first, no goal, timer optional | Sensate-focus grading ([review](https://www.springermedicine.com/sensate-focus-for-sexual-concerns-an-updated-critical-literature/21958914)) | L4 to 6, the summit only for long timers |
| Scene or Persona | Title, one-line setup, a cue word to step out | Perel's "seeing the partner anew" (theory) | L7, the summit |
| Note or Tease (deferred) | A message or note for tomorrow | Extends the afterglow past the night (WNRS final card) | L3 to 5, cool-down and envelope |

Mystery cards and twists are mechanics layered on these formats, not formats themselves. Keep one mystery a night, and make "up" twists feel rare and cooperative, like The Mind's shuriken, which needs every player's agreement ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Mind_(card_game))). "Down" twists can apply automatically, because softening never needs consent.

## Audit the 467-card deck for repetition, voice and endings first

A quick measurement of `shared/content.ts` (counts by level and type, word lengths, and a regex scan for turn-back cues and comparison words) shows a deck that already avoids the riskiest content. The scan found **no exes, grievances or "best ever" comparisons**. The only rating cards rate a week, a pick-up line or the partner's flirting, playfully. The gaps are structural. Levels 1 to 3 hold 51, 40 and 40 cards, but **30, 21 and 20 of them come from templated frames**. Level 1 has 12 variants of "What's your favorite {x}?" and 10 this-or-that variants, which is the "same five themes rephrased" pattern that Paired users notice ([Habi](https://habi.app/insights/best-couple-apps/)). Level 3 talk cards almost never hand the turn back: by the regex count, about 1 in 26 has an explicit swap or "then ask me" cue. Level 7 has only 28 cards, averaging 24.5 words, and 10 of them run past 25 words. Voice shifts from first person at Levels 1 to 3 ("Tell me...", 43 of 51 Level 1 cards) to "your partner" from Level 4 up. On two phones showing the same card, "me" is ambiguous unless the screen names the reader. The ending pools are the starkest gap. With **4 closers, 6 envelopes and 3 twists in each direction**, a weekly couple will see a repeated closer by about week five, in exactly the minutes the peak-end rule says they will remember. Applying the rough depth estimate from the pacing notes (50 to 80 cards per level lasts a weekly couple about three months), Levels 2, 3, 7 and 8 sit below that range, and the template-heavy levels are effectively thinner than their counts.

| # | Check | Passes when | Deck today (measured) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Length | 8 to 20 words; none over 25; extra instructions in a secondary line | L1 to 6 average 13 to 19 words; L7 has 10 of 28 over 25 |

## Conclusion

The research reframes what "more engaging" means for Tandem. The biggest gains will not come from bolder or more numerous questions. They will come from three under-built parts of the experience: the turn-back (every answer gets a response, and both partners take a turn), the reveal (sealed answers and private matches that a paper deck cannot do), and the ending (closers and envelopes that change from night to night and land warmer than what came before). Content can stay suggestive at most because **intimacy and desire share a pathway through feeling responded to**. A warm Level 2 card that makes a partner feel special is a legitimate on-ramp to Level 5, not a detour.

The practical order of work follows from where memory concentrates. First, expand the closer, envelope and twist pools and guarantee one "up" twist and a sealed final card. Second, turn the peak plateau into one summit and cap long timers at one a night. Third, rewrite the template-heavy Level 1 to 3 cards toward "us", reciprocity and varied formats, and trim Level 7 scenes to a title plus one line. Because no study has tested any of this in a couples game, each change should ship with a simple measure that respects house rule 10, such as nights finished as a share of nights started. Watching that total across versions is the closest the project can get to an experiment without collecting anything personal.
