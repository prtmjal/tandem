The 36 questions are a real exercise from a real study, but they were built to create closeness between two people, not to guarantee love. They are a good idea to borrow: take turns, start light, go a little deeper, and listen warmly. Below is what they were, an honest look at what they can and cannot do, and 12 original questions to try tonight.

## Where the 36 questions came from

The questions come from a 1997 paper by the psychologist Arthur Aron and colleagues. The paper was about generating closeness between two people in a lab.

The method is three sets of questions that get gradually more personal. It takes about 45 minutes, and partners take turns answering and sharing. It was designed to create feelings of closeness between strangers, and the study measured closeness. It did not measure lasting romantic love.

The idea became famous in 2015, after Mandy Len Catron wrote a personal essay about it for The New York Times Modern Love column. Since then, "the 36 questions" has been a popular thing for couples to try, and a popular thing to argue about.

## Why the three-set structure works

The shape is the clever part. Each set is a little more personal than the last, so nobody is asked for something big before they have warmed up.

- **Warm-up.** Easy, low-stakes questions. They get you talking, and they remind you that your partner's answers are interesting.
- **Deeper.** Questions about values, memories and what matters to you. By now you have a rhythm of listening and being listened to.
- **Most personal.** Questions that invite honesty and a little vulnerability. They land better after the first two steps than they would as an opener.

Think of it like a walk that starts on flat ground. The idea is that trust builds a step at a time, because each answer that is received kindly makes the next one easier to give. That is a sensible way to think about conversation, and it is the reason the structure is worth copying.

## 12 original questions in the same spirit

Here are four questions for each step. They are written fresh and are not the original list. Take turns, and answer in any order.

### Step one: warm-up

1. What is a small thing from this week that you enjoyed more than you expected?
2. Which meal could you eat again and again without getting bored?
3. What is a skill you would love to wake up having?
4. Which place from your past would you most like to show me?

### Step two: deeper

5. What is something you were told growing up that you still carry with you?
6. When do you feel most like yourself?
7. What is a risk you are glad you took, and what did it teach you?
8. Which friendship or relationship has shaped you most, and how?

### Step three: most personal

9. What is something you would like me to understand about you that is hard to put into words?
10. When have you felt most supported by me, and what did I do?
11. What is a worry you carry quietly, and what would help on a day it feels heavy?
12. What is something you hope for us that you have not said out loud yet?

If a question from step three feels like too much, that is useful information, not a failure. Swap it for another one.

## How to do it well

The questions matter less than how you ask and answer them. A few habits make the difference.

1. **Take turns.** One person answers fully, then the other. The back-and-forth is the point.
2. **No interrupting.** Let your partner finish, even if there is a pause. Silence is often someone thinking.
3. **Say "tell me more".** Follow up before you share your own answer. Curiosity is what makes people feel heard.
4. **Respond warmly.** A smile, a nod or "thank you for telling me that" goes a long way.
5. **Keep an easy pass.** Either of you can skip any question, with no explanation. A pass keeps the whole thing safe enough to be honest.

One step per evening across a week works too.

## Do they work?

It depends on what you mean by "work".

If you mean "do they help two people feel closer for an evening", the idea behind them is sound. Closeness grows from taking turns, being curious and responding warmly, and the structure gives you all three. A calm hour of real attention is a good thing in itself.

If you mean "will they make someone fall in love with you", no. The original study measured closeness between people in a lab, not lasting romantic love. Scientific results from a short lab exercise should not be read as a promise, and a game cannot guarantee love.

Here is a balanced way to hold it:

- **Reasonable to expect:** a pleasant, focused conversation and a chance to hear something new from someone you know well.
- **Not reasonable to expect:** a shortcut past the slow work of trust, shared life and kindness over time.

If an evening of questions leaves you both a little warmer, that is a good result. It does not need to be more than that.

## Frequently asked questions

**Who wrote the 36 questions?**
They come from a 1997 paper by the psychologist Arthur Aron and colleagues, about generating closeness between two people in a lab. They became widely known in 2015 after a personal essay in The New York Times Modern Love column by Mandy Len Catron.

**Do the 36 questions make you fall in love?**
No. They were designed to create feelings of closeness between strangers, and the study measured closeness, not lasting romantic love. A set of questions can open a good conversation, but it cannot promise anything more.

**How long does it take?**
The original method takes about 45 minutes. At home you can stretch it over a week, one step per evening, or stop whenever you like.

**Can I use these with a partner I have known for years?**
Yes. Many couples use this kind of exercise to slow down and listen. Skip any question that does not suit you, and swap in your own.
