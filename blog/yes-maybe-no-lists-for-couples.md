A yes, maybe, no list is a way for two people to rate a set of ideas separately, then compare. Each of you marks every idea as yes, maybe or no on your own, then you look only at where you overlap. It makes honest answers easier, because nobody has to say "no" to your face.

It works for everyday decisions: dates, trips, weekend plans, home routines, ways of spending time together. This post explains the method, why it works, how to run it on paper, and how Nod Twice's match list does the same on two phones.

## The problem it solves

When one partner suggests an idea out loud, the other often feels a small pressure to be agreeable. "Sure, sounds fun" comes out, even when the honest answer is "I would rather not". Or the opposite happens: neither of you suggests anything, because you do not want to be turned down.

Both lead to the same place: plans that fit neither of you perfectly, or no plans at all.

A yes, maybe, no list gets around this in three ways:

- **A private first answer.** You answer before you hear your partner's answer, so you cannot adjust to please them.
- **Three options, not two.** "Maybe" is a real answer. It means "depends on the day, the cost or the mood", and it keeps good ideas alive that a forced yes or no would kill.
- **No is safe.** When a "no" is just a tick in a column, nobody has to defend it. It is information, not rejection.

## What yes, maybe and no mean

Agree on the meanings before you start:

- **Yes:** I would be glad to do this.
- **Maybe:** I am open to it under the right conditions, or I am not sure yet.
- **No:** I would rather not, and I do not need to explain why.

The last part matters most. A no needs no reason. If it comes with a mandatory explanation, it stops being safe.

## How to run it on paper

You need two sheets of paper and a pen each. Ten to fifteen minutes is enough.

1. **Choose a topic.** Pick one area: weekend plans, trips, home routines, date nights, or ways to spend an evening. Closeness can be a topic too, but start with something easy.
2. **Write the ideas.** Together, list 15 to 20 ideas, one per line. Both of you add some. Keep them specific: "a long hike with a picnic" is better than "outdoors".
3. **Copy the list.** Each of you takes a copy and keeps it hidden.
4. **Rate privately.** Next to each idea, write Y, M or N. Do not discuss it yet.
5. **Compare.** Put the sheets side by side. Only look for the overlap.
6. **Read the overlap.** Anything that is Y/Y, Y/M or M/M is a good candidate. Anything with a No from either person is simply off the list for now, with no discussion.
7. **Pick one.** Choose one idea from the overlap and put it in the calendar.

A few tips:

- **Do not ask about the No ideas.** If your partner wants to explain, let them. Do not ask.
- **Re-run it in a few months.** Answers change. A "no" today can become a "maybe" later.
- **Keep the winners.** A running list of ideas you both liked is a handy answer to "what shall we do this weekend?"

## What to put on your lists

Here are some starting points, written to be everyday and easy:

### Dates and evenings

- Cooking a new recipe together
- A board game night
- A long walk with no destination
- A film marathon of one series
- Visiting somewhere neither of you has been

### Trips and weekends

- A weekend away within two hours of home
- A trip with a packed schedule versus a lazy one
- A camping night
- A day trip by train
- Visiting a city for its food

### Routines at home

- A phone-free hour each evening
- A weekly walk
- A shared playlist for chores
- A fixed night for a proper dinner
- Waking up earlier, together, on one weekend morning

### Ways of spending time

- Learning something together, like a class or a language
- Volunteering for a cause you both like
- Trying a new hobby for four weeks
- A regular slot of quiet time side by side

## Why this works for more than choosing activities

The same idea turns up in the [relationship check-in](/blog/relationship-check-in-questions): ask privately, share deliberately, respond kindly. It is also at the heart of games like [who's more likely to](/blog/whos-more-likely-to-questions-for-couples), where each person picks first and then both reveal together.

It is a small habit, but it changes the question from "will you say yes to me?" to "what do we both want?"

## Doing it on two phones with Nod Twice

If paper feels fiddly, Nod Twice has a match list built in. [Nod Twice](/blog/how-a-two-phone-couples-game-works) is a free browser game where each of you plays on your own phone, with no download and no account. Both of you confirm you are 18 or older.

Once in a night, the game shows a short list of ideas. Each of you rates them yes, maybe or no on your own screen. Your partner only sees that you have answered.

When you have both finished, the game shows only the ideas you are both open to, in a shuffled order. It does not show your individual answers, and it does not show the ideas either of you said no to. The raw answers are then forgotten. Progress is saved on your own phones, and you can keep a "next time" list of ideas you were both open to.

It is the same method as the paper version, with the sorting done for you and a bit more privacy built in. If you are on a video call, there is also a mode for couples who are apart. ## Frequently asked questions

**What if we have no overlap at all?**
Add more ideas, or loosen your categories. It also helps to look at the Maybes: ask what would turn a maybe into a yes, such as a shorter time, a lower cost or a different day.

**Is it okay to change a No later?**
Of course. Answers belong to the moment, so you can re-run the list whenever you like. No one has to change an answer to please the other.

**Should we explain our Maybes?**
Only if you want to. A short note like "depends on the weather" can help, but it is optional.

**Can this work for bigger decisions?**
It is best for choices between options, such as plans, trips and routines. For major life decisions, treat it as a starting point for a conversation, and consider a licensed counsellor if you are stuck.
