A good relationship check-in is short, regular and kind. Set aside 15 minutes once a week, start with appreciation, bring up one thing to adjust, and finish with one plan for the week ahead. This post gives you the format and 18 questions to choose from.

The aim is not to audit your relationship. It is to make small things easy to say before they grow into big ones, and to notice what is going well while you are at it.

## Why a weekly ritual works better than a big talk

Many couples only have "the talk" when something has already gone wrong. By then both people are tense, and the conversation has a lot to carry.

A small, predictable slot changes that:

- **Small things get said early.** "I would love a bit more help in the mornings" is easy on a calm Sunday and hard in the middle of an argument.
- **It is normal, not an alarm.** If you check in every week, a check-in does not mean anything is wrong.
- **Good things get noticed too.** Routines tend to hide the things that work. A ritual gives them a spot.

Pick a time that is already easy: a walk, a Sunday tea, the first fifteen minutes after dinner. Keep it away from bedtime, when tired people say things they do not mean.

## How to keep it from becoming a performance review

This is the part that matters most. A few ground rules make a check-in feel safe:

1. **Use a timer.** Fifteen minutes, then stop, even if you are mid-flow. A fixed end makes it easier to begin.
2. **Speak for yourself.** "I felt rushed on Tuesday" lands better than "you always rush me".
3. **One adjustment each, not a list.** If you have five complaints, pick the one that matters most this week.
4. **No scorekeeping.** Do not bring up old events as evidence. Stay in the last seven days.
5. **Listen without fixing.** When your partner shares something, say it back first: "so the evenings felt flat?" Then respond.
6. **Pause is allowed.** Either of you can say "let's stop here and come back tomorrow". That is a plan, not a failure.
7. **Skip a week if you need to.** The ritual works for you, not the other way round.

## The format: appreciation, one adjustment, one plan

Here is a simple three-part shape. About five minutes each.

### Part one: appreciation (5 minutes)

Each of you names things you valued this week. Starting here sets the tone, and it makes the harder part easier, because you are both reminded of what is working.

Choose from:

1. What is one thing I did this week that you appreciated?
2. When did you feel closest to me this week?
3. What made you laugh this week?
4. What is something small I did that you noticed?
5. What is one thing you are proud of yourself for this week?
6. What went well for us as a team this week?

### Part two: one thing to adjust (5 minutes)

Each person offers one thing, framed as a request. Your partner listens, repeats it back, and says what they can offer.

1. What is one thing that would make next week easier for you?
2. Was there a moment this week you felt unheard or rushed?
3. Is there something I do that you would like a little less of, or a little differently?
4. What is one thing you would like more of from us?
5. Is anything sitting unsaid that you would like to get off your chest?
6. How is the balance of chores, plans and rest feeling right now?

If a topic is bigger than five minutes, name it, and book a separate time for it. Do not try to solve it at the check-in.

### Part three: one plan for the week (5 minutes)

End on something concrete and enjoyable.

1. What is one thing we want to do together this week, even something tiny?
2. What is one thing each of us needs this week: time alone, an early night, help with a task?
3. Is there anything coming up that we should coordinate now?
4. What would you like to hear from me on a tough day this week?
5. What should we look forward to next weekend?
6. Is there a small treat we want to plan, like a walk, a meal or a film?

Write the plan down in one sentence. "Walk on Saturday morning; I will do the shopping on Thursday so you can rest." That is your whole check-in.

## Variations

- **Short version, five minutes:** one appreciation each, one request each, one plan. Do it over coffee.
- **Check in by message:** if schedules clash, swap your answers in a chat, then talk for five minutes in person. Our post on [games to play with your partner over text](/blog/games-to-play-with-your-partner-over-text) has ideas for keeping messages playful.
- **Check in from far away:** keep the same format on a call. [Long-distance relationship games](/blog/long-distance-relationship-games) and [FaceTime games for couples](/blog/facetime-games-for-couples) help when the call needs a spark.
- **Write first:** both of you jot answers privately, then read them out together. This helps if one of you tends to go along with what the other says.

## Frequently asked questions

**How often should we do a relationship check-in?**
Weekly works well for many couples because the events are fresh. If that is too much, every two weeks is fine. Pick a rhythm you will keep without dreading it.

**What if one of us does not want to do check-ins?**
Offer a five-minute version and make it about appreciation first. If it still feels uncomfortable, ask what would make it easier, such as a walk instead of a sit-down.

**What if a check-in turns into an argument?**
Stop, take a break, and agree when to come back. You can also keep the topic for a calmer moment. If conversations often turn painful, a licensed counsellor can help you find a better way to talk.

**Do we need a script?**
No. Choose two or three questions from each part, or just use the three headings as a guide.
